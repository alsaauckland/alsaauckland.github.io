Executive Recruitment · Helpful Information

Applying to the exec, demystified.

Applying to become an ALSA executive is a real process, and a deliberately thorough one. This walks you through what each stage involves, so there are no surprises and you can put your best foot forward.

The process is designed to see how you think and how you work, not to catch you out. It also deliberately mirrors a real clerkship application, from the written questions to the video and the interview, so going through it here is a genuine, low-stakes rehearsal for the recruitment you will face when you apply to firms. You get to experience what that process feels like, and get better at it, before it counts. The steps are the same for everyone, and the effort is deliberate: the wrong person in a role costs the team more than an empty seat, so we ask for a real application and give real consideration in return.

What We're Looking For

The short version.

We are not looking for the most polished CV or the person who has done the most. We are looking for people who follow through, who are honest about where they are, and who genuinely care about the work. Everything below is really just different ways of showing us that.

  • Follow-through over flash. Someone who reliably does what they say matters more to this team than someone with an impressive list they cannot back up. Every stage is a small test of whether you will actually do the thing.
  • Genuine interest in this specific work. We can tell the difference between "I want a committee role for my CV" and "I care about this portfolio and here is why." The second is what we are hiring for.
  • Self-awareness and honesty. Knowing what you are good at, what you are still learning, and being straight about both. You do not need to have it all figured out. You need to be real about it.
What You'll Submit

The application, by track.

Officers

A CV, a short video, written questions, and a task relevant to the role, followed by an interview. Officers own a portfolio and represent ALSA to members and partners, so the video lets us see how you communicate and carry the organisation, not just what you have done before.

Marketing team (Social Media Officer and Designer)

A CV, written questions, and a portfolio of your work submitted with the application, plus a task relevant to the role, followed by an interview. No video for these roles: the work speaks for itself, so your portfolio and the task are where you show what you can produce. If you do not have a formal portfolio yet, a simple folder or document with samples of what you have made is completely fine.

Interns

A CV, written questions, and an interview, with no task or video. The intern tier is about potential, not experience, so the ask is lighter by design.

The 2027 round is aimed at recruiting Officer and Marketing team roles. Interns and cabinet are not part of this intake, but the intern track is described here for reference.

Your CV and Written Answers

The written part.

  • Keep it concrete, one to two pages. Lead with your most recent and most relevant experience, and describe it in terms of what you actually did and what changed because of it. "Member of the debating club" tells us nothing; "ran the club's schedule and grew turnout from 10 to 30" tells us how you operate. Specifics are what let us picture you doing the role.
  • Answer the actual question that is asked. The written questions are not a formality, they are the main way we get to know how you think. Read each one carefully, answer the thing it is actually asking, ground your answer in a real example rather than generalities, and respect any word limit. Going over, or answering a slightly different question than the one asked, both read as not paying attention.
  • Show why ALSA, and why this role specifically. "I want to get involved" is not an answer, because it could be said about any club. Tell us what draws you to this particular portfolio, what you would want to do with it, and what you would bring. Referencing our actual work, or a gap you think you could help with, shows you have done more than skim the page.
  • Be honest about where you are. You do not need a CV that already impresses, and first and second years are not expected to have one. What we are reading for is whether you want it and can back that up with something real, however small. Ambition plus honesty beats a padded list every time.
  • Do not overinflate. Everything you write can and will be questioned at interview, so only claim what you can stand behind and explain in detail. If you say you "led" something, be ready to talk through exactly what you did. Describing a smaller thing honestly always beats overselling something that falls apart the moment we ask a follow-up.
  • Proofread it, then have someone else read it. This is a written role application to an organisation that produces written work, so typos and sloppy formatting cost more here than in most places. Reading it aloud catches clunky sentences, and a second reader catches the errors your own eyes skip.
The Video

Your recorded answer.

Officer applicants record a short video, around two minutes. The marketing team does not do this. There is one prompt:

Introduce yourself, tell us why you want this role, and give one example of something you have done that shows you can do it.

A simple structure to cover in the time:

  • Who you are. Name, year, and degree, plus a line on what you are about. Keep it to about fifteen seconds.
  • Why this role. What draws you to this portfolio and to ALSA. Be specific to the role, not "I want to get involved."
  • Your proof. One concrete example, a project you ran, a responsibility you held, or a time you delivered. Say what you did and what it shows about you.
  • Why you. Close with a sentence on what you would bring. Warmth beats polish.

On delivery: a quiet space with a tidy background and light in front of you, look at the camera rather than yourself, slow down, and do one practice run and watch it back before you record for real.

The Task

A preview of the real work.

Officer and marketing applicants complete a short task relevant to the role. It is the closest thing to a preview of the job itself, and it is where we learn the most about how you would actually operate.

The task should take no longer than three hours in total, spread across three days. It is designed to fit around a full study load, so if you find yourself pouring far more time into it than that, step back: we are testing judgement and follow-through, not who can grind the longest.

  • Treat it like the job, not an exam. The task mirrors something the role genuinely involves, so approach it the way you would approach real work for the team: understand what is being asked, make sensible calls, and produce something you would actually be comfortable handing over.
  • Show your thinking, not just the output. We care as much about how you got there as what you produced. A short note on your reasoning, the trade-offs you weighed, or what you would do next with more time, tells us more than a polished result with no thought shown behind it.
  • Follow the brief and hit the deadline. Doing what was actually asked, on time, is the first and most important thing we are looking for, because it is the whole role in miniature. A brilliant answer to a different question, or a great piece of work handed in late, both miss the point.
  • Done and clear beats perfect and overworked. Within the three hours, aim for something complete and easy to follow rather than something you have polished endlessly. Knowing when good enough is good enough is part of the skill.
The Interview

Meeting the team.

The interview is a conversation, not an interrogation. We want to understand how you think, whether you would be good to work alongside, and whether the role genuinely fits you. Smart casual is right for it.

  • Know your own application cold. Anything in your CV, written answers, or task is fair game, and we will often dig into the specifics. Reread what you submitted beforehand and be ready to talk through any of it in detail, because this is where overstated claims come unstuck and honest ones shine.
  • Prepare for the classics, with real examples. Expect some version of "why ALSA", "why this role", and "tell us about a time you led or delivered something". Have genuine stories ready rather than rehearsed lines, and be ready to talk about what you actually did, what was hard, and what you learned. Specifics are far more convincing than polished generalities.
  • Bring a couple of real questions. Thoughtful questions about the work, the team, or how the role is changing show genuine interest and that you are weighing this up seriously too. Avoid asking things the website already answers, which signals the opposite.
  • Be a person, not a performance. We are deciding whether we want to work alongside you for a semester, so we are as interested in what you are like as in what you have done. Trying to sound like a lawyer already tends to land worse than being warm, honest, and yourself. It is completely normal to be nervous, and it is fine to take a moment to think before you answer.
Common Questions

FAQs.

Do I need previous exec or legal experience?
No. Officers need to show they can plan and execute; interns are chosen on potential. Non-legal work, leadership, and involvement all count. Show what you have done and what it taught you.
Which track should I apply for?
Officers own a portfolio, and it is natural for second years and above to step into that kind of leadership. Interns support an officer and suit first and second years and international students, no experience needed. The 2027 round is recruiting Officer and Marketing team roles.
Can I apply for more than one role?
This round, you can choose up to two roles. Pick the two that fit you best, and make clear which is your first preference.
How much time does a role take?
Officer roles are roughly 5 to 10 hours a week, with more in event weeks. Each role page sets out the specifics.
When do applications open?
Applications for the 2027 executive team open soon. Follow us on our socials to hear the moment they do.
