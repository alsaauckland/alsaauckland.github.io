Auckland has one of the most capable cohorts of Asian law students in the country. ALSA was built to make sure that capability translates into outcomes. Not in ten years, not once the profession figures it out on its own, but now, while you are still here.

Our programmes are built around one idea: the skills that matter most in a legal career are rarely taught in a law school classroom. How to walk into a networking event and leave with a genuine connection. How to write with precision and authority. How to present yourself in an interview at a firm that does not yet know your name. We run workshops on all of this. We connect students directly to law firm partners, barristers, and practitioners who are looking for talent. We facilitate semester-long mentorships with people who have been through the process. And through this newsletter, we put the work and voices of Auckland's Asian law students directly in front of the people who make hiring decisions.

ALSA is a professional, student-led association. We run events, and we run them well, but this organisation is not defined by a social calendar. It is defined by results. Every student who lands a clerkship, makes a strong connection, or finds their footing in this profession through ALSA is exactly why we built this.

Issue 1 is the beginning. Welcome to it.

Jayden LinFounding President, ALSA Auckland

Mentoring

Mentoring Programme

Cayla Huang & Charlotte Dobbs  ·  Mentoring Officers

Hi ALSA. We are Cayla and Charlotte, your Mentoring Officers for 2026. Our portfolio is the ALSA Mentoring Programme: a structured, semester-long pairing between law students and practitioners that we are launching this year for the first time. Here is what it looks like.

The ALSA Mentoring Programme is one of the flagship initiatives we are building in 2026. The goal is to give Auckland law students direct access to legal professionals who have been through the clerkship process, the career decisions, and the early years of practice and who are willing to share what they actually know.

The programme runs through Semester 2, from July to October. Applications are open to Part II, Part III, and Part IV students. Part II students are welcome to apply and will be considered for selection if their following year is their penultimate year of study. This is a semester-long relationship, not a one-time coffee chat. Mentors will provide ongoing guidance through the semester, helping mentees understand how the profession works, what strong clerkship applications look like, and how to make decisions about their career with better information than most students have access to.

Mentors are matched based on shared interests and similarities in background and career direction. We aim for two mentees per mentor, though this will depend on each mentor's capacity and what they are genuinely able to commit to. The programme is built around quality over volume. For mentors, it is an opportunity to invest meaningfully in the next generation of Auckland lawyers. For mentees, it is access to guidance that is specific, honest, and relevant to where you actually are in your degree. The 2026 programme is semester-long by design. As the programme matures and we build the mentor network, we intend to expand to a full academic year from 2027 onwards.

If you are a legal professional interested in becoming a mentor, we want to hear from you. Mentee applications will open at the start of Semester 2. Full details on the application process, what the programme involves, and how pairings work will be in Issue 2.

Development

Development

Kevin Anand & Daniel Dizon  ·  Development Officers

Hi ALSA. We are Kevin and Daniel, your Development Officers for 2026. Our portfolio covers three things: networking events that connect members with legal professionals, the Professional Spotlight feature in every issue of this newsletter, and the Legal Careers Board where we publish open roles and opportunities from our law firm partners. Together, these are the channels through which we build ALSA's presence in the profession and make sure our members are visible to the right people at the right time.

One thing we want to be clear about from the start: our networking programme is not built exclusively around large commercial law firms. That would be the easy version of this, and it would not serve most of our members well. The legal profession is broader than the firms that recruit on campus in March, and an LLB opens far more doors than most students are shown during their degree.

We are deliberately building connections across the full range of where Auckland lawyers actually work. That means boutique and specialist practices, including firms of five to fifteen lawyers doing excellent, sophisticated work in employment, immigration, resource management, family law, and criminal defence. It means in-house legal teams at banks, insurance companies, iwi organisations, and government departments, where the work is often more varied and the client contact more immediate than in private practice. It means Crown Law, the Public Defence Service, community law centres, and roles in the public sector that most students do not discover until well after graduation. And it means barrister's chambers, where sole practice and direct brief work look entirely different from anything else in the profession.

The reason we are building this breadth is straightforward. Most students arrive at clerkship season having been exposed almost exclusively to large commercial firms through on-campus events, law school career panels, and the firms that happen to advertise loudly. That is not a complete picture of the profession, and making a career decision from an incomplete picture is not a good starting point. Whether you end up in a large firm or not, you should understand what the full range of options actually looks like: what the work involves day to day, what the culture is like, what the career trajectory looks like, and whether it fits who you are and where you want to go. Our networking events are built around giving you that understanding before you commit to a direction.

Each event connects ALSA members directly with practitioners across different areas of practice. The aim is not simply to get your name in front of a hiring partner. It is to give you a genuine map of the profession.

Academic

What We Are Running This Year

Amber Sue & Tri Phan  ·  Academic Officers

Hi ALSA. We are Amber and Tri, your Academic Officers for 2026. Our portfolio is built around one conviction: the skills that matter most in a legal career are rarely the ones the curriculum formally teaches. Law school teaches you doctrine. It teaches you to read cases and absorb statutes. What it does not reliably teach is how to retain that doctrine under pressure, how to structure a response that actually answers the question being asked, how to communicate across cultural registers that define much of Auckland's legal market, or how to present yourself to a profession that makes judgements quickly and on limited information. Those are the gaps we are built to close.

Each workshop in our 2026 programme is designed around a specific gap. They are run with practitioners, not just by students. The goal is not to supplement your degree. It is to prepare you for what comes after it.

ALSA: Learning to Learn

Most students who struggle academically are not short on effort or intelligence. They are short on method. This workshop is built for students who feel like they are putting in the hours but not seeing the results they expect, and for students who are performing well but want to understand why, so they can do it more deliberately.

We work through how to structure a reading session, how to distinguish what is being examined from what is merely being taught, and how to build a body of legal knowledge that holds up under pressure rather than fragmenting the night before an assessment. The second half of the workshop moves directly into assessment performance: how to structure a legal answer that demonstrates your reasoning rather than just reciting it, how to apply doctrine to a problem without over-explaining it, and how to use the time available without wasting any of it. Law school rewards precision and structure. This workshop works through exactly what that means in practice, using real exam questions, common structural mistakes, and the specific techniques that separate a solid answer from an excellent one. If you have ever walked out of an exam knowing the material but doubting your answer, this is for you.

Cultural Competency Workshop

As New Zealand becomes increasingly diverse, a significant portion of legal instructions, client relationships, and professional interactions will involve cultural norms, communication styles, and relationship expectations that differ meaningfully from the defaults most New Zealand lawyers operate from. These dynamics are almost never addressed in a law school context. Yet they shape how trust is built, how instructions are given, and how long-term professional relationships are maintained across the full range of Asian communities present in Auckland's legal market. This workshop works through those dynamics with practitioners who navigate them daily. It is one of the more distinctive things ALSA offers, and one of the more professionally relevant, regardless of which area of practice you end up in.

Networking Workshop

Most students walk into their first networking event without a plan. They leave with a handful of business cards and a vague sense that something should happen next. Nothing usually does. This workshop covers what the curriculum does not: how to approach someone you do not know and make the conversation worthwhile, how to make a meaningful impression without it feeling transactional, how to follow up in a way that builds a relationship rather than ending one, and how to identify the people in a room worth speaking to before you have spoken to anyone. Facilitated by legal professionals who have built their careers on doing this well and who are willing to be candid about what actually works, and what most students get wrong.
