The Founding Story

You should not have to shrink to succeed.

ALSA Auckland began as an idea in December 2025, and by May 2026 it had become a reality. The same pattern had kept emerging. New Zealand is one of the most diverse countries in the world. Auckland is one of the most diverse cities. And yet the further up the legal profession you look, the less that diversity is reflected. The pipeline narrows at every stage. It is a structural outcome that accumulates over years, and one the profession itself has begun to name. ALSA was built to address it from the student end.

Many students are exceptional. What they are missing is not ability, but infrastructure: visible pathways into the profession, mentors who have walked the same road, a professional community that understands where they have come from and where they are trying to go. ALSA was built to provide exactly that: the visibility, the relationships, and the professional structure that turns capability into career.

What students need is a platform. A community that shows them they can stand in the same rooms as anyone else in the profession, not despite who they are, but because of it. A place where their background is not something to manage around, but a genuine professional asset. Where they can see that others have walked the same path and made it through.

ALSA was built to change those conditions directly. It is an independent incorporated society, not a cultural club. It exists as a professional platform: structured, credible, and anchored in the skills and relationships that actually move people forward in the profession.

Within three months of launching, ALSA had gathered 200 members, built a social media following of over 500, and run its first events to capacity.

"The lawyers we are building relationships with today are the colleagues, the mentors, and the advocates we will rely on for the rest of our careers."

Jayden Lin, Founding President
The Founding

Built from the ground up.

ALSA did not arrive fully formed. It was built by three people who committed to the vision before there was anything to show for it.

Founding President

Jayden Lin

ALSA began as an idea in December 2025. Jayden had noticed something unspoken: there was no platform connecting Asian law students to each other or to the profession. The more he looked, the more he saw capable students held back not by ability, but by the absence of visibility and access. So he built something.

Within months, ALSA had a constitution, a 17-person executive, government incorporation, over 200 members, and sponsorship partnerships with major brands including Anytime Fitness. In April 2026, ALSA held its Inaugural Launch Event with Mai Chen as headline speaker.

The Evidence

The numbers tell a consistent story.

ALSA's founding is grounded in evidence, not anecdote. The research on Asian communities in Aotearoa's legal system is clear: the gap between representation at entry level and representation in senior positions is real, persistent, and structural.

A documented collapse in representation

The NZ Law Society's Snapshot of the Profession 2024 records Asian lawyers at 11.9% of the profession overall, and 19.4% at junior level. Down from 22.7% the year before. Asian students enter law in strong numbers. The opportunity is to ensure that entry-level strength translates into long-term careers. That is precisely what ALSA is built to support.

Source: NZ Law Society Snapshot 2024 →

The Law Society's own findings

The Law Society's own publications acknowledge "still significant barriers to admission, progression and retention within the legal profession" for Asian lawyers. A 2023 NZ Asian Lawyers stocktake reported that members had experienced unconscious bias and cultural barriers in the workplace. These are findings the profession has produced about itself and is now actively working to address.

Source: NZLS & NZAL findings →

Cross-cultural competence as a professional necessity

Mai Chen's report on Culturally and Linguistically Diverse Parties in the Courts, supported by the Ministry of Justice and the Law Foundation, found that Auckland is now more superdiverse than London or New York. The report recommended cross-cultural communication training for lawyers and expanded teaching at law schools, citing the Melbourne Asian Law Centre as a model for the kind of institutional infrastructure New Zealand needs.

Source: Mai Chen, Ministry of Justice Report →

The profession is responding

In November 2023, Chief Justice Dame Helen Winkelmann joined NZ Asian Lawyers and the Law Society for a joint seminar on the issues faced by Asian lawyers and law students. The conversation is happening at the highest levels of the profession. ALSA is the student-facing response to it, built to operate in parallel with the structural work the profession is already beginning to do.

Source: LawTalk Issue 956 →

The Vision

One chapter is only the beginning.

ALSA Auckland is just the beginning. The vision does not stop here. The goal is to build a unified national network across New Zealand, with shared resources, shared speaker relationships, shared standards, and a professional community that extends well beyond any single university.

The longer-term ambition is to establish New Zealand as a national chapter of ALSA International. That connection would open direct pathways for New Zealand's Asian law students: international competitions held across Asia, exposure to how law is practised in different Asian jurisdictions, direct engagement with Asian law firms operating internationally, and the kind of cross-border relationships that are increasingly relevant in a legal market becoming more globally connected every year.

New Zealand's legal profession has an opportunity to be genuinely fluent in the legal and commercial cultures of its largest trading partners and fastest-growing communities. ALSA is building the students who will make that possible for the next generation.
