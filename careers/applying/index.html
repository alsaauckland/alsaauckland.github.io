Applying to firms, demystified.

Clerkship and graduate applications can feel like a black box, especially if you are the first in your circle to go through it. This is a plain-English guide to how the process usually works and how to put your best foot forward at each stage.

Every firm runs its process a little differently, and the details change year to year. Treat this as a general map, not a rulebook, and always check the firm's own careers page for the specifics. The habits below travel well across almost any application.

The Shape of It

How the process usually runs.

Most commercial firms follow a version of the same five stages. Timing clusters around set clerkship and graduate windows each year, so the biggest early advantage is simply knowing when applications open and having your materials ready before they do.

01
Online application
Submit your CV, academic transcript, and written answers through the firm's portal. Some firms use short essay or behavioural questions instead of a cover letter.
02
Online assessments
Often a recorded video interview and one or more online tests: logical or verbal reasoning, and sometimes interactive competency or judgement games.
03
Interviews
Usually one or two rounds, often with partners or senior lawyers. Expect a mix of questions about you, your interest in the firm, and how you think.
04
Offers and outcomes
Firms make contact, often by phone, within a set window. Cohorts typically start at a fixed point in the year.

Dates and formats vary by firm and change each year. Confirm the current timeline on the firm's own careers page before you rely on anything here.

The Written Application

Your CV and written answers.

  • Lead with substance, not length. One to two pages. Recent, relevant experience first, and make achievements concrete: what you did and what changed because of it.
  • Have your transcript ready. Most firms ask for it. If a grade needs context, address it briefly and factually rather than hoping it goes unnoticed.
  • Answer the actual question. Where firms use essay or behavioural questions instead of a cover letter, answer what is asked, give a specific example, and keep to the word limit.
  • Show genuine interest in that firm. A generic application reads as generic. Reference the firm's work, values, or people in a way that could not be copy-pasted to a competitor.
  • Proofread, then have someone else read it. Typos in a legal application do more damage than in most fields. A second reader catches what you cannot.
Video and Online Assessments

Recorded interviews and tests.

Video interviews record your answers to set questions, usually with a short time to prepare and a limit to respond. They feel unnatural because they are, so practice matters.

  • Set the scene. A quiet space, no interruptions, a tidy neutral background, and good light in front of you rather than behind.
  • Look at the camera, not yourself. It reads as eye contact. Sit up, slow down, and treat it like a real conversation.
  • Structure your answers. A quick point, a specific example, then the result. Better to make one clear point well than to ramble toward three.
  • Do a proper practice run. Record yourself once and watch it back. It is uncomfortable and it works.
  • For reasoning tests, warm up. Free practice tests exist online. You are training familiarity with the format as much as the content, so do a few before the real thing.
Interviews

Meeting the firm.

  • Know your own CV cold. Anything on the page is fair game. Be ready to talk about any of it with specifics, not slogans.
  • Prepare for the classics. Why law, why this firm, why commercial, a time you led or handled conflict. Have real examples rather than rehearsed lines.
  • Use structure under pressure. For behavioural questions, situation, what you did, and the outcome keeps you clear when nerves hit.
  • Bring questions. Thoughtful questions about the work, the team, or the training signal genuine interest. Avoid asking what the website already answers.
  • Dress to the firm's cue, usually business or smart business casual. If unsure, slightly more formal is the safer error.
  • Be a person, not a performance. Firms are deciding whether they want to work with you. Warmth and honesty go further than trying to sound like a lawyer already.
After You Apply

Outcomes, feedback, and next time.

  • Ask for feedback if it is offered. Not every firm gives it, but where they do, it is the fastest way to improve for the next round.
  • A no is not a verdict on you. Cohorts are small and competitive. Strong candidates miss out every year and go on to excellent careers.
  • Most firms let you reapply. A stronger application in a later window is common and welcomed. Treat this round as data, not a door closing.
Common Questions

FAQs.

When do applications open?
Clerkship and graduate windows cluster at set points in the year and differ by firm. Check each firm's careers page and diarise the dates early, because the window can be short.
Do my grades have to be perfect?
No. Grades matter, but firms look at the whole picture: experience, involvement, communication, and fit. Strength across the application can outweigh a single mark.
What do I wear to an interview?
Business or smart business casual, matched to the firm's cue. If you are unsure, err slightly more formal.
I have no legal work experience yet. Does that rule me out?
No. Firms know where you are in your degree. Non-legal work, leadership, and involvement all demonstrate the skills they are looking for. Show what you have done and what it taught you.
