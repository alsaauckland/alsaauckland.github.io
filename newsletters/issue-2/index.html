Cultural Competency Workshop

Amber Sue & Tri Phan  ·  Academic Officers
11 August 2026

ALSA ran its Chinese Cultural Competency Workshop at the Institute of Professional Legal Studies, facilitated by Mingze Sun, barrister at FortyEight Shortland.

Cultural competency is a skill the profession increasingly asks for, but formal training in it remains limited, and most practitioners are left to pick it up on their own. The session was not what the title tends to suggest. Much of it was about restraint. Working through Deng v Zheng [2022] NZSC 76, Mingze took students through the Supreme Court's caution that evidence a cultural practice exists is only a starting point. There must be evidence connecting that practice to the actual parties and their conduct. The more generalised the cultural evidence, the greater the risk of stereotyping. Cultural context does not displace the ordinary legal framework, but properly evidenced, it can help explain conduct a court might otherwise misunderstand.

The second half was practical: how cultural considerations shape client communication and decision-making, why a client's approach to settlement may be driven by considerations beyond the immediate financial outcome, and how understanding those considerations changes the conversation you have with them.

Thank you to Mingze for giving his time, to IPLS for hosting us, and to our Academic Officers, Tri Phan and Amber Sue, for organising the evening. If you want to be notified when the next workshop opens, make sure you are signed up as an ALSA member.

Asian Lawyers in Practice: The Career Journey

With CLC, KLSA & SALSA  ·  Hosted by Buddle Findlay
25 August 2026

On 25 August, ALSA joined Asian Lawyers in Practice: The Career Journey at Buddle Findlay's Auckland office, alongside CLC, KLSA and SALSA. The evening brought together students and legal practitioners for a discussion on the realities of working in law, pathways into practice, and the experiences that shape a career beyond university.

The session was built around the career journeys of a panel of Buddle Findlay lawyers. Panellists spoke about entering the profession, the decisions and opportunities that shaped their careers, and what the transition from law school into practice actually looks like.

A significant part of the evening focused on the experiences of the panellists as Asian individuals within the legal profession. They reflected on cultural identity, the challenges they encountered, and how their backgrounds have informed their experiences in practice.

The panel also addressed the challenges of a career in law, emphasising that professional development is rarely linear. For students, the evening provided valuable exposure to the profession, practical perspectives on career decisions, and an opportunity to engage directly with practitioners.

ALSA extends its thanks to Buddle Findlay, CLC, KLSA, SALSA, the participating panellists and students for contributing to an evening of thoughtful discussion, professional connection and shared experience.

NZYAL Mix and Mingle

Hosted by New Zealand Young Asian Lawyers
13 August 2026

On 13 August, New Zealand Young Asian Lawyers (NZYAL) hosted a Mix and Mingle networking event at Panama Social, bringing together ALSA and the other Asian law student clubs, CLC, KLSA and SALSA, alongside law students, graduates, and practitioners from across Auckland's legal community. The relaxed, social setting made for an ideal environment to connect, with attendees ranging from first-year law students to experienced solicitors and barristers keen to share their career journeys.

Throughout the evening, conversations touched on everything from navigating clerkship applications and choosing a practice area, to balancing cultural identity within the legal profession. Several NZYAL committee members were on hand to share their own experiences breaking into the industry, offering practical advice and encouragement to those just starting out.

For many students, the event was a valuable reminder of the strength and warmth of Auckland's Asian legal community, a space where connections form organically over a drink and a shared plate, rather than in a formal legal setting.

Thank you to NZYAL for creating opportunities like this, allowing students to build real connections and a sense of community that can shape the trajectory of a young lawyer's career. ALSA looks forward to continuing to support and collaborate with NZYAL on future events.

Inaugural Mentoring Programme: Opening Night

Cayla Huang & Charlotte Dobbs  ·  Mentoring Officers
5 August 2026

ALSA's Inaugural Mentoring Programme officially launched with a successful opening night hosted at DLA Piper on 5 August. The event brought together senior Asian law students and legal practitioners from a range of practice areas, providing an opportunity for mentors and mentees to establish professional connections and lay the foundation for their mentoring relationships throughout the programme.

The evening provided mentees with valuable exposure to the legal profession beyond the university environment and they had the opportunity to engage directly with practitioners, learn about their experiences, and gain insight into the transition from law school to legal practice. It created space for open and meaningful conversations and highlighted the value of mentorship in providing students with guidance and perspectives that complement their goals and experiences.

ALSA extends its sincere thanks to Max Kwan and DLA Piper for generously hosting the launch of the mentoring programme and to all mentors for their time, commitment, and contribution to the programme.

As the relationships continue to develop throughout the remainder of the academic year, the programme provides students with ongoing support, guidance, and professional development. Further opportunities and upcoming events will bring the mentoring pairs together again to keep building these meaningful connections throughout the semester.

Precedent: The Launch of the ALSA Podcast

Hosted by our Founding President

A law degree can lead almost anywhere, but students rarely get to hear what that path actually looks like once they leave university. That's the gap that ALSA's new podcast series, Precedent, is built to close. Hosted by our Founding President, each episode sits down with someone who started in law and asks where it actually took them.

The debut episode featured Rayhan Langdana, Senior Associate in Russell McVeagh's litigation team. Rayhan's own path has taken him from India through the UK and Ireland to New Zealand, and later from a Russell McVeagh school leavers' scholarship into the firm's litigation team, time as a junior barrister at Shortland Chambers, and three and a half years at Quinn Emanuel in London, before returning home.

The conversation reflected on the transition from high school to university and learning to self-motivate without external marking, lessons from his first summer clerkship, the contrasts between practising in New Zealand and the UK, and how AI is reshaping firms' expectations of junior lawyers.

ALSA thanks Rayhan for a generous and genuinely candid first conversation, and IPLS for sponsoring the episode. Members can watch the full episode on ALSA Auckland's YouTube channel.

The next episode turns to a different kind of path: a law graduate who's now co-founding an AI startup. Stay tuned to ALSA's channels to keep up to date with the podcast.

In Their Words

Development Officers

Every issue, we take a moment to recognise the people going beyond what's expected of them. That means two spotlights: a current student showing real leadership and contribution to the community outside the classroom, and a graduate lawyer whose early career work deserves a wider audience. This issue, that's Matthew Lee on the student side and Max Kwan on the graduate side.

Student Spotlight
Matthew Lee
University of Auckland · NZLSA Witness Examinations National Champion

I had an unusual start to my law degree. I switched one of my conjoint degrees in my third year to study law, so I was a bit older than everyone else when I started.

Recently, I got the opportunity to represent the University of Auckland at the New Zealand Law Students' Association and compete in the Witness Examinations competition. Earlier in the year, I won the regional competition and was a semi-finalist at the Australian Law Students' Association. I'm really excited to share that I became the New Zealand national champion for Witness Examinations. The conference was held in Wellington over the course of four intense days: five preliminary rounds over two days and the Grand Finals at the historic Old High Court. My competitors really were the best of the best. I competed against some of them in Australia so I knew that I had my work cut out for me at NZLSA. With only ninety minutes to prepare, including thirty minutes to brief my witness, the Witness Examinations competition is a real test of quick thinking, multitasking, and knowledge of the law. Ultimately, I was able to walk away with the win.

Something interesting I noted at the conference was that I was the only person of colour (and specifically only person of Asian descent) in the Witness Examinations competition. I left the conference thinking that there is still a lot of work to do to make the legal field more culturally diverse.

My advice for new students in law, especially those who might feel a little out of place, is to go to events, join clubs, and do competitions! It's the best way to make friends at law school, and you should never underestimate the power of your connections at law school. Not only do they make great study buddies, but these are the people you will be spending your career with. Get to know them and you'll find that there is so much to learn from each other.

Graduate Spotlight
Max Kwan
Associate, Tax Team · DLA Piper New Zealand · University of Auckland alumnus

I'm Max Kwan, a recently promoted Associate in DLA Piper's New Zealand Tax Team and an alumnus of the University of Auckland. Long before becoming a lawyer (or even starting law school) I spent much of my childhood and teenage years working at my parents' fruit and vegetable shop (for free). Like many children of immigrants, and as the oldest sibling, I was often tasked with interpreting government documents, translating correspondence and helping my parents navigate unfamiliar systems.

Looking back, those experiences shaped a lot of how I work today. The shop taught me how to communicate with people from all walks of life, build relationships, and develop a strong work ethic. Helping my parents with paperwork taught me how to break down complex information, identify the key issues, and work under (immense) pressure.

Today, I advise clients ranging from multinational companies to high-growth New Zealand businesses on tax and transactional matters. While the subject matter is very different, commercial law is ultimately a people business. As with selling fruit and vegetables, providing legal services requires you to be able to build relationships, effectively communicate, and have a genuine desire to solve problems. My recent promotion to Associate has been a rewarding milestone and I credit much of it so far to skills which can't be taught in a lecture theatre. My advice to students is to make the most of the opportunities that exist during law school both inside and outside the classroom, as you may never know which of the experiences or skills you develop will end up having the largest impact on your career.
